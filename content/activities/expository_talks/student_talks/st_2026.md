## EP2611: Optimization for Machine Learning

- **Speaker:** Vishal Vikram Parth (M.Math 1st year), ISI Delhi
- **Abstract:** This talk presents a broad overview of optimization for machine learning, bridging theoretical foundations with practical algorithms. We begin with the basic geometry of optimization: global and local optima, gradients and Hessians, stationarity, first-order necessary conditions, and the classification of stationary points via curvature. Convexity, L-smoothness, and strong convexity are introduced as key structural assumptions, along with their implications for convergence and global optimality. We then derive gradient descent from the steepest-descent direction and discuss step-size selection, termination criteria, stochastic and mini-batch gradients, and the empirical loss setting. The talk covers acceleration and adaptation methods, including momentum, Nesterov accelerated gradient, AdaGrad, RMSProp, and Adam, as well as regularization techniques such as L2, Adam with L2, and AdamW. Additional topics include learning-rate schedules, Newton's method, and logistic regression. Finally, we turn to constrained optimization, Lagrangian duality, KKT conditions, and their application to hard- and soft-margin support vector machines, including the dual formulation and the kernel trick. The goal is to provide a unified view of the optimization tools that underlie modern machine learning.
- **Pre-requisites:** Recommended that attendees already have familiarity with the subject; run as a crash course in cohort with Co-Labs.

## EP2610: Fundamental Groups: An Introduction to Algebraic Topology

- **Speaker:** Muhammad Shameel (M.Math 1st year), ISI Kolkata
- **Abstract:** This talk introduces the fundamental group as an introduction to algebraic topology. We begin with motivating problems in topology like: is there a map from B^n to B^n without any fixed points? Is there a retraction from B^n to S^n? Is R^n ≅ R^m for m ≠ n? These problems are hard to tackle using just point-set topology; using fundamental groups we can resolve these questions for 2 dimensions. After defining the fundamental group, we develop covering spaces to calculate the fundamental group, listing key properties of covering spaces and discussing its relation to the fundamental group and its subgroups. We then discuss further properties of the fundamental group, including its behavior under homotopy equivalence and the extent to which it captures the topology of a space. We look at a more analytical way to calculate the fundamental group using van Kampen's theorem, which computes the fundamental group of a space from those of simpler open pieces covering it, and we conclude by applying it to determine the fundamental groups of all orientable surfaces.

## EP2609: 'The Random Variable is Roughly Its Expectation' — The Kahn-Kalai Conjectures

- **Speaker:** Atmadeep Sengupta (M.Stat 1st year), ISI Kolkata
- **Abstract:** Let X be a finite set and Xp denote its random subset, where each element is included independently with probability p. Consider any increasing property of Xp (e.g., the containment of arithmetic progressions of length r, or the containment of consecutive integers). For |X| = n and p = p(n), a value p̂ is defined as the threshold if p = o(p̂) implies P(Xp has the property) → 0, and p = ω(p̂) implies P(Xp has the property) → 1. The first Kahn-Kalai conjecture proposes an elegantly computable bound for this threshold. The second Kahn-Kalai conjecture focuses on a special case for random graphs, suggesting an even stronger bound. In this talk, we will explore the importance and broader implications of both conjectures. Additionally, we will walk through a short proof of the first conjecture — due to Tran and Vu — and examine exactly why this proof does not imply the second.
- **Pre-requisites:** Probability 1 & 2, Basic Graph Theory (recommended, but not compulsory), and a healthy dose of patience to bear with the speaker!
- **References:**

  2. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.17207
  3. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.02144

## EP2608: Regression Techniques

- **Speaker:** Lavanis A
- **Abstract:** Linear regression is one of the fundamental statistical tools for predictive analysis, providing a framework for understanding relationships between variables and making predictions based on historical data. This work introduces the key concepts and assumptions underlying linear regression and examines situations in which it is an appropriate modelling technique. The discussion begins with simple linear regression, including estimation and inference for model parameters, and extends to multiple linear regression. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) is introduced to assess the significance of regression models, followed by measures of goodness of fit, including R² and adjusted R². The limitations of linear regression are then examined, particularly in situations where the response variable is binary or the assumptions of the linear model are not appropriate. This motivates an introduction to logistic regression as a classification framework for binary outcomes. The logistic regression model is developed through the estimation and interpretation of its parameters, with emphasis on modelling the probability of an outcome taking the value 0 or 1. Overall, the work provides a foundation in regression-based predictive modelling, covering both continuous and binary response settings.

## EP2607: What is Haar Measure?

- **Speaker:** Srijeet Bhattacharjee (M.Math 1st year), ISI Kolkata
- **Abstract:** The Lebesgue measure on R has more properties than an abstract measure. Specifically it has certain approximation properties and is translation invariant, which makes the measure compatible with both the topology and group structure on R. We would investigate Haar measure which is a generalization of these two properties in the setting of an abstract topological group. This makes it an indispensable tool in Harmonic analysis and probability theory.
- **Pre-requisites:** Measure theory, group theory, topology (only basic ideas, even Metric spaces suffices).

## EP2606: From Random Walks to Heat Kernels: An Introduction to Markov Chains

- **Speaker:** Aritrabha Majumdar (M.Math 1st year)
- **Abstract:** Markov chains provide a fundamental framework for describing stochastic systems whose future evolution depends on the present state but not on the past. This talk develops an elementary introduction to both discrete-time and continuous-time Markov chains, with particular emphasis on the connections between the two settings. We begin with transition matrices, the Chapman--Kolmogorov equations, simple random walks, stationary distributions, and convergence to equilibrium in finite state spaces. We then pass to continuous time by introducing exponential holding times and jump rates, leading naturally to the infinitesimal generator and the Kolmogorov equations. Finally, we interpret the transition kernel as a matrix exponential, connecting Markov-chain evolution with semigroup theory and the heat equation. The goal is to develop a unified picture of Markov chains in which transition matrices, generators, equilibrium distributions, and heat kernels arise as different descriptions of the same underlying stochastic evolution.
- **Pre-requisites:** Idea of conditional probability, expectation, variance, discrete and continuous random variable (and their pmf and pdf), linear algebra, notion of matrix exponential, notion of convergence in metric spaces.

## EP2604: Smallest Volume Hyperbolic Link Complement with n Components

- **Speaker:** Shashank Gupta, IISER Mohali
- **Abstract:** In 2001, Cao and Meyerhoff proved that the smallest volume hyperbolic link complement with one cusp is the figure-8 knot complement. Agol posed a generalisation of this as a question: what is the smallest volume hyperbolic link complement with n components? In his thesis, Venzke proposed an answer to Agol's question, arguing that the volume for n ≥ 11 is beaten by the (n−1)-fold cyclic cover of the Whitehead link complement. But this was without proof until Kaiser–Purcell–Rollins proved Venzke's statement for n ≥ 60. In this talk, I will discuss the original conjecture of Agol, which states that minimally twisted chain links are the smallest volume hyperbolic n-cusped manifolds. The Kaiser–Purcell–Rollins paper provides computational evidence that they are beaten by the Whitehead cyclic cover, but this is not a complete proof showing that the chain link is globally minimal volume among all n-cusped manifolds in these ranges. Even though most data support this numerically, a complete classification or proof is still open for some of these small n. Also, a complete classification of minimal volume n-cusped manifolds for 3 ≤ n ≤ 59 needs to be given. I will discuss all the possibilities related to this open problem. The talk will start with some preliminaries from hyperbolic knot theory and then lead to this open problem. It may be useful for people who want to work in the vast domain of 3- and 4-dimensional geometry and topology.
- **Pre-requisites:** Basic knowledge of knot theory, Hyperbolic Geometry.

- **Date and Time:** Friday (14th August), 4:30 PM to 5:45 PM.

## EP2602: A probabilistic proof of Cayley's Theorem using Poisson Branching process

- **Speaker:** Ahan Chakraborty (B. Stat, 2027)
- **Abstract:** Cayley's formula establishes that the number of distinct labeled trees on $n$ vertices is exactly $n^{n-2}$. While classical proofs traditionally rely on combinatorial bijections with Prufer codes, double counting, or algebraic graph theory approaches, this talk presents an alternative probabilistic proof by modeling tree generation through a Galton-Watson branching process with a Poisson offspring distribution.
